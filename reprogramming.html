Evidence library / Epigenetic reprogramming

Partial cellular reprogramming: can cells regain youthful function?

Reprogramming factors can reverse some age-associated cellular changes in experiments. The question is whether that can be translated into safe, meaningful human treatments.

First-in-human trial underwayClinical benefit: unprovenExperimental gene therapy
Evidence profile · Selective editorial review · Updated 9 October 2026
The evidence verdictPartial reprogramming has produced striking changes in cells and promising functional effects in mice. An early human safety trial has begun. Its sponsor reported interim observations from only three participants after 56 days; these results have not established efficacy in a controlled trial. It is not yet known whether this approach can safely restore human tissue function, prevent age-related disease, or extend lifespan.

What is partial cellular reprogramming?

Scientists can alter a cell’s identity by expressing certain transcription factors, often called Yamanaka factors: OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 and c-MYC (OSKM). Full reprogramming can return mature cells to a stem-cell-like state. Partial reprogramming aims to stop earlier, potentially reversing some age-associated changes while preserving the cell’s specialised identity.

Some experiments use three factors (OSK), leaving out c-MYC. Different factors, tissues, delivery methods and exposure schedules may have very different effects; they should not be treated as one proven intervention.

Landmark research

2016 · Mouse models

Short pulses of reprogramming factors changed aging-related traits

Ocampo and colleagues reported improved age-associated features and longer survival in a genetically engineered mouse model of premature aging. They also observed improved tissue recovery in some normally aging mice.

How to interpret it: Lifespan extension in a premature-aging model does not establish lifespan extension in normal mice or people.

Ocampo et al. · Cell · 2016
2020 · Mouse vision and optic nerve

Reprogramming factors restored aspects of visual function in mice

Lu and colleagues used three factors (OSK) in mouse retinal ganglion cells. The experiments reported changes in DNA methylation patterns, improved axon regeneration and better visual performance in models of glaucoma and aging.

How to interpret it: A result in mouse eye tissue cannot be assumed to apply to other organs or to human vision.

Read source ↗
Lu et al. · Nature · 2020
2022 · Naturally aging mice

Molecular rejuvenation signals in tissues

Browder and colleagues investigated longer-term partial reprogramming in physiologically aging mice and reported shifts in several age-associated molecular measures.

How to interpret it: Changes in molecular age-related signatures do not necessarily mean longer life or better clinical outcomes.

Browder et al. · Nature Aging · 2022
2026 · Human Phase 1 · Interim company report

ER-100 enters clinical testing for optic neuropathies

In an 8 October 2026 company announcement, Life Biosciences reported that three people with open-angle glaucoma tolerated the first tested dose through day 56, with preliminary changes in visual-field measurements in two. These were sponsor-reported interim observations, not results of a completed controlled efficacy trial. The trial is designed primarily to assess safety and tolerability.

How to interpret it: Only three participants, no control group, 56 days of follow-up, and sponsor-reported interim data. Changes in visual-field testing can be influenced by measurement variability and repeat testing. These observations do not establish effectiveness or human rejuvenation.

Read source ↗
Life Biosciences · 8 October 2026 · NCT07290244

What the human announcement does—and does not—show

The study is primarily designed to evaluate safety and tolerability in a specific eye condition. A recommendation to test a higher dose does not establish effectiveness or long-term safety. Even a confirmed improvement in glaucoma would not demonstrate rejuvenation throughout the body.

Why safety is central

  • Cell identity: Excessive reprogramming could cause cells to lose their normal specialised functions.
  • Tumour risk: Uncontrolled reprogramming and pluripotent-cell formation raise concerns about abnormal growth and teratomas.
  • Delivery and control: Gene delivery, tissue targeting, immune responses and the ability to regulate factor expression need careful testing.
  • Long-term effects: Short follow-up cannot reveal all delayed harms or show durable benefit.
  • Biomarkers versus outcomes: A younger-looking epigenetic profile does not automatically mean fewer illnesses, restored function or longer life.

What would change the verdict?

Independent, adequately controlled human trials showing durable improvements in meaningful clinical outcomes, supported by longer-term safety follow-up. Success treating a specific eye disease would be significant, but it would not by itself prove whole-body rejuvenation or human lifespan extension.

Editorial transparency: This is a selective evidence profile, not a systematic review. The 8 October 2026 ER-100 observations come from a sponsor announcement, not a completed, independently replicated, controlled efficacy trial. The announcement is linked directly and must not be mistaken for peer-reviewed evidence of rejuvenation. Sources are linked so readers can examine the original research.

Educational information only; not medical advice. Partial cellular reprogramming is experimental and not a validated self-treatment.
