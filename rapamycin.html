Rapamycin & mTOR: compelling mouse evidence, unresolved human benefits

Rapamycin can extend lifespan in mice. Does altering the mTOR pathway improve healthy aging in people?

Human trials conductedHuman longevity benefit: unprovenPrescription medicine
Evidence profile · Selective editorial review · Updated 9 October 2026
The evidence verdictRapamycin has unusually strong and reproducible lifespan-extension evidence in mice. Human trials show that mTOR inhibition can affect selected biological and immune measures, but no trial has demonstrated that rapamycin extends human lifespan or reliably improves overall healthspan in otherwise healthy adults.

What is rapamycin?

Rapamycin (sirolimus) is a prescription drug that inhibits the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, especially the mTORC1 signalling complex. This pathway helps cells respond to nutrients and growth signals, regulating protein synthesis, metabolism and processes including autophagy.

mTOR signalling is essential for normal physiology. The effects of inhibiting it depend on dose, timing, tissue and treatment duration. Findings for rapamycin must also be distinguished from studies of related drugs such as everolimus.

Landmark research

2009 · Multi-site animal experiment

Rapamycin extends lifespan in genetically diverse mice

In a study across three research sites, rapamycin increased survival in male and female mice even when treatment began relatively late in life. At the age when 90% of animals had died, treated mice lived 14% longer (females) and 9% longer (males) than controls.

What it tells us: A reproducible mammalian lifespan effect—not evidence of the same result in humans.

Harrison and colleagues · Nature · 2009
2014 · Randomised human trial · Everolimus (not rapamycin)

Improved influenza vaccine responses in older adults

A short course of the mTOR inhibitor everolimus (RAD001) improved influenza vaccination responses by approximately 20% at selected doses in older volunteers.

What it tells us: The pathway can be manipulated to affect an age-relevant immune outcome. It does not demonstrate broad rejuvenation, and the study used a related drug rather than rapamycin itself.

Mannick and colleagues · Science Translational Medicine · 2014
2018 · Small placebo-controlled human pilot · Rapamycin

Feasibility in generally healthy older adults

In a trial of 25 adults aged 70–95, eight weeks of treatment did not produce clinically significant changes in the measured laboratory, physical performance or cognitive outcomes. The small study was primarily informative about short-term feasibility and tolerability.

Read the original study ↗
Kraig and colleagues · Experimental Gerontology · 2018
2025 · PEARL trial · 48 weeks · Rapamycin

Intermittent rapamycin in adults aging normally

The double-blind placebo-controlled PEARL study analysed 114 participants who completed the trial. Its primary outcome—visceral fat—did not improve significantly. Selected secondary and subgroup measures, including lean tissue mass and pain outcomes in women, showed exploratory signals; these do not overturn the negative primary outcome. Reported adverse-event rates were broadly similar between groups during the study.

Important limitations: Subgroup findings require replication, the study was not powered to demonstrate longer life, and compounded rapamycin had lower bioavailability than expected. Short-term tolerability does not establish long-term safety. All seven listed authors disclosed being employees and shareholders of AgelessRx, which supported trial administration and publication; this financial interest should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Moel and colleagues · Aging · 2025 · NCT04488601

How to interpret the PEARL results

The trial did not meet its primary visceral-fat endpoint. Subgroup and secondary-outcome findings can generate hypotheses but are more vulnerable to chance findings, especially when many comparisons are explored. Replication by independent groups is needed before treating them as established healthspan benefits.

Risks and limitations

  • Immunological effects: Rapamycin is used clinically as an immunosuppressant. Risks vary with indication, dose and regimen.
  • Known adverse effects: Mouth ulcers, infections, impaired wound healing, altered blood lipids and glucose regulation, low blood counts and other serious effects may occur.
  • Drug interactions: Rapamycin has clinically important interactions and requires professional oversight in approved uses.
  • Unknown longevity regimen: There is no established dose or schedule proven to slow aging in healthy people.
  • Surrogate outcomes: Changes in biomarkers, vaccine responses or body composition do not automatically imply longer life or reduced disease burden.

What would change the verdict?

Independent, adequately powered trials demonstrating sustained improvements in meaningful clinical outcomes—such as fewer age-related illnesses, better physical function or reduced disability—together with robust long-term safety data. Lifespan extension in humans remains a separate and much harder claim to establish.

Editorial transparency: This is a selective evidence profile, not a systematic review. It separates results for rapamycin from related mTOR inhibitors and identifies the PEARL authors’ disclosed commercial interests. Research continues, and the assessment should be revisited as additional trials report.
